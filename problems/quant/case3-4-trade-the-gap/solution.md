### Reference answer

> In model terms, the error-correction term is about −3 %: WTI is roughly 3 % below where its long-run relation with Brent puts it (log spread 0.090 against an average of 0.059). Historically, gaps like this have closed: the error correction model says about 11.5 % of a deviation disappears per business day, so the half-life is about six days. If the relationship holds, the expected path is to recover roughly half of the 3 % within a week and most of it within three — but that is an average; any single episode can widen first.
>
> The statistics stand on an economic mechanism — the export and transport arbitrage between US and seaborne crude — so the first question is why the gap opened. If it's a temporary WTI-specific disruption and the arbitrage route is open, the convergence logic applies. If the gap reflects a constraint on the arbitrage itself — a pipeline or export bottleneck, a regulatory change — the equilibrium may have moved and the "gap" may be the new normal.
>
> What the model cannot guarantee: β and α are estimates; Engle-Granger assumes one stable relationship over 2016–2019 and doesn't protect against a structural change, which is exactly the scenario where this trade loses. Before sizing anything I'd check that the relationship and its adjustment speed are stable across sub-periods.
>
> As a trade, it makes sense only if the expected convergence — about 1.5 % over a week — clearly exceeds transaction costs, roll and carry on the two legs, if the position is sized against the spread's typical daily noise, and if there's a pre-agreed exit if the spread keeps widening, because widening is also what a regime change looks like at the start. I'd present it as "historically, deviations of this size closed by half in about a week", not as a guaranteed profit or a tested strategy.

### What makes this a strong interview answer

- **Translates statistics into the trader's units**: % cheapness, days to half-life, expected move over a week.
- **Uses each coefficient for what it means**: β (where equilibrium is), α (how fast), noise around the average path.
- **Mechanism first**: asks why the gap opened and whether the arbitrage is available.
- **Separates three risks**: estimation (β, α), model (one stable relation, low-power test), regime (the equilibrium itself moves).
- **Turns it into a decision rule**: expected move vs costs, sizing, exit — without overselling.

### Common weaknesses

- "Cointegrated, so it will converge" — treating a statistical description as a guarantee.
- Quoting α without translating it into time.
- Ignoring that a widening spread is also how a structural break begins.
- Claiming a P&L: the analysis is a historical description, not a backtest.
