### Reference answer

> Brent and WTI are both light, sweet crudes that refiners can largely substitute for one another. If WTI becomes cheap enough relative to Brent — after transport and quality differences — it pays to ship US crude abroad (or for refiners to switch), which pushes the two prices back together. So the link is an **arbitrage on the spread**: Brent usually trades at a premium reflecting the cost of moving crude from Cushing to the coast and across the Atlantic, and that premium can move, but it cannot drift away indefinitely.
>
> In the data I'd therefore expect daily returns to be strongly correlated, and the price levels to wander together, with a spread that fluctuates around a typical value and keeps coming back to it.
>
> The link can weaken when the arbitrage is physically blocked: pipeline or storage bottlenecks at Cushing, export restrictions, a freight shock, or a regional supply event such as a hurricane hitting US Gulf infrastructure. Then the spread can blow out and stay wide for a while — the relationship holds by regime, not forever.
>
> Coffee is the control: there is no mechanism linking it to crude. Both are trending prices, so if a regression of coffee on Brent in levels looks "significant", that tells us how much the method can be fooled by shared trends — any link we claim for Brent and WTI must pass tests that coffee fails.

### What makes this a strong interview answer

- **Mechanism before statistics**: arbitrage, transport cost, substitution — with the direction of the trade.
- **A falsifiable prediction**: correlated returns and a spread that mean-reverts — which is precisely what Cases 2 and 3 test.
- **Limits of the mechanism** (logistics, regulation, events) — this anticipates Case 4.
- **Understanding the control series**: a good researcher deliberately includes a series that *should* fail.

### Common weaknesses

- "They're both oil, so they're correlated" — no mechanism, no prediction.
- Expecting a constant spread or a correlation of exactly one.
- Ignoring that the relationship can change when the arbitrage route changes.
